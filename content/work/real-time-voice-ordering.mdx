## The Problem

Phone orders had exactly two modes: wait on hold for a person, or
fight through a touch-tone menu that couldn't handle "actually, make
that a large, no wait, two of them." Neither is what anyone actually
wants at 11pm on a weeknight. The bar for a voice system here wasn't
"sounds impressive" — it was "doesn't make someone hang up and order
somewhere else."

## The Approach

The scope was deliberately narrow: take an order, not have a
conversation about the menu's philosophy. That narrowness is what made
a three-week timeline realistic.

  rationale="Validating the order incrementally — item by item, as the customer speaks — means a misheard item gets caught and corrected in the same turn, not discovered after the customer has already hung up thinking the order was right."
  alternative="Transcribe the whole call, then parse a final order at the end. Simpler to build, but any single misrecognition anywhere in the call became a wrong order with no chance to catch it in the moment."
/>

## What Shipped

Barge-in — being able to interrupt the system mid-sentence the way you'd
interrupt a person — was the single feature that made this feel like a
conversation instead of a transcription pipeline with a voice attached.
Without it, every correction ("no, small, not large") had to wait for
the system to finish talking first, which is exactly the rigid,
frustrating pattern the whole project was trying to replace.

<Callout type="insight">
  Latency budget was treated as a design constraint from day one, not a
  performance pass at the end — every turn had a hard ceiling before it started
  to feel like a delay a caller would notice, and every component in the
  pipeline was chosen against that budget, not against "which is most accurate
  in isolation."
</Callout>

## Retrospective

Three weeks was enough because the scope stayed narrow — order-taking,
not general conversation. The honest limitation is exactly there: this
doesn't handle a caller asking about allergens or store hours mid-order
gracefully, because that was explicitly out of scope, not because it's
architecturally impossible. If this needed to expand into "handle
anything a caller might ask," the planner/specialist split from the
support copilot project is the right next architecture — this version
doesn't have it because the problem, on purpose, never asked for it.
